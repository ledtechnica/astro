Why we replaced our Kubernetes stack with three boring boxes

Eighteen months of cluster rot, a runaway bill, and the day we ssh'd into hardware again.

Maya Ortiz May 4, 2026 14 min read infra

In April of 2024 we ran 60 nodes across three Kubernetes clusters in two regions. We had three SREs on call. Our infra bill was the second-biggest line item in the company. Today we run six servers, on rails, in two colocation facilities. One person is on call. Our bill is one twelfth what it was. Here's how, and the parts that didn't go to plan.

The old stack

For context: we were a Series B startup with twenty engineers and a product that was, frankly, a single Postgres database, two API services, and a handful of cron jobs. We ran it on EKS because that's what you do.

Our setup looked something like this — three clusters for prod, staging, and a "scratch" environment we'd let ourselves get talked into, plus a managed Postgres, plus a managed Redis, plus a managed Kafka we'd added six months earlier for one specific feature and then never removed.

module "prod_cluster" {
  source  = "terraform-aws-modules/eks/aws"
  version = "~> 19.0"

  cluster_name     = "prod"
  cluster_version  = "1.27"
  node_groups      = local.node_groups  // 18 groups, do not ask
}

The breaking point

The thing nobody tells you about a managed Kubernetes setup is that it's only managed up to a point. Past that point, you own a distributed system. We owned a distributed system that was running, generously, four containers worth of actual product code.

The breaking point came on a Sunday. A node-group autoscaler bug — karpenter@0.34, in case you're curious — started churning nodes every three minutes. Each new node spent ninety seconds pulling images. Pods stayed pending. Our API served 503s for forty-one minutes before someone got woken up.

The thing we were paying for had failed in a way only the thing we were paying for could fail.

Three boring boxes

The replacement plan was simple, almost embarrassing:

  • One physical server for the web tier (nginx + our Go binary)
  • Two physical servers in primary/replica for Postgres
  • Cloudflare in front for TLS, caching, and DDoS

We picked dedicated Hetzner boxes because the price was indecent and they ship with sane defaults. The whole topology fits on a napkin. The whole runbook fits in a single markdown file.

The deploy script

We replaced Argo, Flux, Helm, and a hand-rolled GitHub Actions workflow with this:

# deploy.sh — replaces ~400 lines of yaml
set -euo pipefail
GOOS=linux go build -o ./bin/api ./cmd/api
rsync ./bin/api web-01:/srv/api/api.new
ssh web-01 "mv /srv/api/api{,.old} && \
                 mv /srv/api/api{.new,} && \
                 systemctl restart api"

What broke

Three things, in order of how much they cost us:

  1. Postgres failover. Our first replica setup used streaming replication with manual promotion. The first real failure was at 2am on a Wednesday. The DBA was asleep. We lost twenty-six minutes. We now use Patroni and it has not failed us once in eight months.
  2. Email deliverability. Turns out Hetzner IPs have a reputation problem. We moved transactional email to a managed provider in the second week.
  3. Our own habits. We kept reaching for kubectl. The muscle memory took a month to retrain.

When this is wrong

If you're running anything that needs to elastically scale ten-to-one in a few minutes, this doesn't work. If you have hundreds of services, this doesn't work. If your traffic is truly unpredictable, this doesn't work.

It worked for us because our actual workload — a couple thousand requests per second, two TB of data, a handful of jobs — fit comfortably on a single nineties-era idea of a server. Most workloads do. Most workloads aren't most workloads.
